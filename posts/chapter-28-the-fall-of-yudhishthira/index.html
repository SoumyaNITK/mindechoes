Chapter 28 - The Fall of Yudhishthira

2025-09-04

The grand hall of Hastinapura shimmered with gold pillars and chandeliers. Courtiers, elders, princes, and noble guests had gathered, their eyes filled with curiosity and unease. In the middle sat the beautifully polished dice board—innocent in appearance, but hiding a storm.

Yudhishthira, the King of Indraprastha, walked into the hall with his brothers. Calm, noble, and dressed in simple royal garments, he bowed respectfully to the elders: Bhishma, Drona, Kripacharya, and Dhritarashtra.

But behind the welcoming smiles of Duryodhana and Shakuni, there was a dark plot.

Yudhishthira was hesitant. “Gambling brings ruin. But if invited to play in a royal court, how can I refuse? Dharma is my guide, and I must not dishonor this hall.”

A throne was placed for him. Across the board sat Shakuni, the uncle of Duryodhana, rolling the dice on behalf of the Kauravas. Yudhishthira played for the Pandavas. The game began.

At first, small wagers were placed. Yudhishthira lost. Again, and again. Every time Shakuni threw the dice, he smiled slyly. He had practiced trickery all his life. His dice obeyed him.

But Yudhishthira, true to his word, didn’t back down. “I will not stop midway. A Kshatriya does not leave a challenge unfinished.”

The Game Escalates

He started wagering gold, silver, jewels, elephants, horses, and entire cities.

He lost them all.

The court murmured, but nobody dared to intervene. Bhishma and Drona watched in silence. Even Vidura, who was deeply disturbed, warned Dhritarashtra, “This is adharma. Stop this now!” But the old king, blinded by love for his son, remained silent.

Yudhishthira's Descent

Then, in desperation or madness, Yudhishthira wagered his brothers—first Nakula, then Sahadeva.

He lost.

Then he staked Arjuna—the mighty warrior of Bharat. And lost.

Then came Bhima—the strongest man in the kingdom. The one who had vowed to crush Duryodhana’s thighs.

He lost Bhima too.

Now he had nothing left.

Duryodhana smirked. “You still have yourself, cousin.”

Yudhishthira closed his eyes. Silence fell upon the court.

With trembling hands, he said, “Very well. I stake myself.”

The dice clattered. Shakuni smiled.

“Lost again.”

Yudhishthira was no longer a king.
He was now a servant. A slave. A broken man.

The Pandavas had been defeated—not in battle, but on a board of dice, through deceit. The hall was heavy with shame. But it was not over.

Duryodhana laughed cruelly. “You still have one possession left. One more jewel. One more thing that belongs to you.”

Yudhishthira looked up in horror.

“Your wife. Draupadi.”
